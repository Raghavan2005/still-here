Still Here

Finding people who cannot ask for help, in the first days after a disaster

Problem analysis and proposed solution (2-pager) · Prototype: see link supplied with this document

Still Here

1. The problem and why we chose it

After a mass disaster, half the population may be gone and the rest are shaken, short of food and connectivity. Help goes to people who can ask: those with a phone, signal and a voice. Some of the most vulnerable cannot ask at all: a baby alone in a flat, a bedridden elder, a person with a disability, someone whose carer has vanished. Someone else usually knows they exist: a neighbour, a relative in another city, a nurse with a patient list. That knowledge has nowhere to go.

Why this one. We compared several gaps (supplies, shelter, family tracing, rumours). We chose this because (a) the cost of delay is highest: a child without water has hours, not days; (b) it is the gap existing channels miss, since social posts and helplines reward people who can speak for themselves; (c) it is solvable with ordinary people, because a neighbour within walking distance is the only resource that exists on Day 1; (d) it has a hard design tension worth solving: the same address that saves a child also invites a thief or kidnapper. We therefore built the product around who sees what, and when.

2. Who it is for

  • Primary: the responder, a neighbour who is shaken, on foot, with one hand free and a few spare hours. If responders do not turn up nothing else matters, so their screens get the most care.
  • Reporters: a relative far away, a nurse, a neighbour behind a locked door. They use one short report form.
  • Never a user: the person in need. They cannot use an app. Every screen is written about them, not to them.
  • Care staff and local groups: vouch for helpers and see cases that nobody has claimed.

3. The product and how it solves the problem

A light web app (plain files, nothing to download) that runs one loop: report → someone goes → they record what is needed → someone is named as responsible. Everything else is deliberately left out.

4. Key decisions and what we rejected

  • Rejected: a public map. Fastest to build, but it is a target list.
  • Rejected: verify before a report counts. A delay costs lives; consequences after the fact keep reports honest without slowing the first one.
  • Rejected: free notes at the end of a visit. Replaced with a mandatory owner and next-check time.
  • Rejected: app-store install or login. Anyone with a basic browser, even offline, must be able to use it.

We do not count downloads, time in app or reports submitted: more use is not more safety.

6. Risks and limits (honest view)

  • A determined thief can still lie their way in; staging, logging, vouching and the second-person rule reduce but do not remove this.
  • Volunteer supply is the real bottleneck; the product exposes it but cannot create helpers.
  • The prototype is a visual representation only: data is simulated and not connected to live services.
